Simulated USD/JPY

Paper book only. The line below is a classroom rule named short-cover-reading. It is labelled simulated and educational. It is not a live order, not a broker fill, and not a prediction.

Before and after

Lesson anchor 160 yen per dollar

Scenario mark 159 yen per dollar

The anchor is the whole number in the article’s phrase “above 160”. The article does not say the rate was exactly 160.00. The scenario mark is that anchor plus a classroom constant of −1.00 yen. The constant is round so the arithmetic is easy to check. It was not estimated from the Bank of Japan, the ECB, or TradingView. The article does not say where USD/JPY went after the remarks.

Paper result for one illustrative book. Money in this table does not exist.
Item Value
Pair USD/JPY
Paper book 10,000 US dollars measured against yen
Lesson anchor 160 yen per dollar
Classroom constant −1.00 yen per dollar
Scenario mark 159 yen per dollar
Yen result 10,000 × (159 − 160) = −10,000 yen
Dollar result −10,000 ÷ 159 = −62.89 USD when rounded to the cent

In desk jargon this book is long dollars and short yen: the yen result is negative when the scenario mark is lower. The side exists so the arithmetic has a sign. It is not a position to copy, and it is not a suggestion to hold dollars or yen. Dollars are converted at the scenario mark. That is bookkeeping for the lesson.

A lower USD/JPY is the usual way a “short-covering” reading would show up: traders who had already sold yen later repurchase yen to close that position, and the pair prints lower. This page describes that habit. It does not tell you to repurchase yen or dollars.

Step 3

Tutor

Explanation only. This section cites the same Mainichi article. It does not tell you to buy or sell, and it does not name a level to aim at.

Why this page drew the line lower

The Mainichi article, dated November 13, 2026, reports two ideas that pull in different directions. The sandbox illustrates only one of them, and it labels that illustration as a reading of the words.

The article says the yen was under renewed selling pressure because people expected the Federal Reserve to step up rate hikes, which the article says would widen the interest-rate gap between the United States and Japan. That is the article’s context for a weaker yen, which would be a higher USD/JPY. PrismOS does not turn that sentence into a path.

The article also quotes Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama. She said Japan will take appropriate action if the yen’s depreciation requires it, after “sharp, one-sided” moves, and after the dollar climbed above 160 yen in New York trading overnight, a level the article says had not been seen since early September. She said, “We will respond appropriately.”

A common reading of a warning like that is short covering: traders who had already sold yen later repurchase yen to close the position, in case the authorities act. That repurchase would show up as a lower USD/JPY. The simulated path is a picture of that habit, one yen lower than the article’s round “160”, so you can see the sign of the paper result. The article does not say this happened. It does not say Japan intervened. It does not name a rate the government wants.

Check the official panels before you treat the picture as history. Tokyo 17:00, the ECB’s 14:15 CET euro fixings, and New York overnight are different clocks. If a panel says the article’s date is missing, believe the gap.

How to check the source

  1. Open the Mainichi article. Confirm the date line and the Kyodo credit. If the wording differs from this card, trust the page.
  2. Separate the headline from the quote. The headline is the paper’s summary. The quotation marks are what the article attributes to Katayama.
  3. List what the quote does not say. It does not confirm an intervention. It does not give you a price. It does not instruct a reader.
  4. Open the Bank of Japan series and the ECB series from the links in this page. Compare dates before you compare numbers.
  5. If the TradingView frame is blank, leave it blank. Do not type a price from somewhere else into this lesson.

Pause before you trust it

Pattern to notice: a named official, a round number (160), and urgent wording (“sharp”, “one-sided”). Real wire copy looks like that. So do posts that want you to act before you think.

Three questions are enough to slow down: Who published it? What is the date? Which sentence is a quote, and which sentence is missing?
